Does Drinking Chlorophyll Water Clear Acne?

BIO 311C · Biology Myth Buster · Fall 2026

The Myth Our Team

📱 The Trend

It became a dietary trend on social media, especially TikTok, to drink water that contained chlorophyll. Influencers and others claimed it was a detoxifier and an anti-acne elixir that would completely stop breakouts.

This was such a huge topic on social media because acne is the most common skin disease. It affects about 85% of teenagers and young adults. These types of trends where there is a revolutionary solution to a problem such as acne are highly consumed by viewers. On social media, there were over 56 million posts that had the hashtag associated with skin care and chlorophyll. This leads to the spread of false information or information with no evidence to prove it; opinions are treated as facts.

🔬 Scientific Evidence

🥤

The first piece of evidence we observed was if drinking chlorophyll led to any improvement in people’s acne. There are zero clinical trials that support oral chlorophyll supplements in treating acne. Digestion breaks down molecules before they reach the skin tissue in meaningful amounts (Ferruzzi and Blakeslee). Any of the improvements that were shown are from the increased intake of water and hydrating, not the chlorophyll that was added (Akdeniz et al.).

🧴

The second piece of evidence we observed was using chlorophyll in a topical sense. This was done through gels and cream. A 3-week pilot trial showed certain gels did in fact improve acne lesions (Stephens et al.). This is just preliminary support, however, and isn't solid enough to make the claim that chlorophyll clears acne.

💡

The third piece of evidence we observed was photodynamic therapy. This was a randomized trial where they combined a gel that contained chlorophyll and red light that helped clear acne (Song et al.). This does provide clinical support that drinking chlorophyll clears acne.

🧩 Research Gaps

We found there are 4 key research gaps within this claim. These 4 claims are the absence of oral trials: there are completely zero clinical trials proving that drinking chlorophyll works. It is most likely just a placebo effect. The second gap we found was: scientists do not know whether ingested chlorophyll can survive stomach acid and digestion to even reach the skin (Ferruzzi and Blakeslee). The third gap we found was that all the study sizes were very little with little to no repetition (Stephens et al.; Song et al.). These claims don’t follow the rules of the scientific method and therefore can’t be trusted. The last gap we found was the mix-up with water. Even when there are improvements, researchers can’t confirm whether the improvements are directly from the added chlorophyll or just the increased water intake (Akdeniz et al.).

✅ Our Verdict: Myth Busted

There is no scientific evidence that drinking chlorophyll water clears acne. Chlorophyll applied to the skin shows some early promise, but more research is needed.
